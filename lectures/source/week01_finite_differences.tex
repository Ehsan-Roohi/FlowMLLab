\topic{7}{Finite differences: from a slope to a formula}\label{sec:fd-slope}
A computer stores finitely many samples of a continuous field. Finite differences turn derivatives into algebraic combinations of these samples. We derive the formulas, measure their errors, and assemble a Poisson equation.

Use a uniform grid $x_i=x_0+ih$, with $h=x_{i+1}-x_i$, $f_i=f(x_i)$, and $f'_i=f'(x_i)$. A prime means differentiation, not an iteration. A derivative is a limiting slope, $f'(x_i)=\lim_{h\to0}[f(x_i+h)-f(x_i)]/h$. On a grid, $h$ is finite. We approximate the derivative using available samples and ask how the neglected terms depend on $h$. A difference is the numerator; a derivative approximation also divides by the correct distance.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=2.4cm,y=1cm,>=stealth]
\draw[->] (-.35,0)--(2.5,0) node[right] {$x$};
\foreach \x/\lab in {0/{i-1},1/i,2/{i+1}}{
\fill[accent] (\x,0) circle (2pt);
\node[above=5pt] at (\x,0) {$f_{\lab}$};
\node[below=5pt] at (\x,0) {$x_{\lab}$};}
\draw[<->] (0,-.9)--(1,-.9) node[midway,below] {$h$};
\draw[<->] (1,-.9)--(2,-.9) node[midway,below] {$h$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\textbf{What is a Taylor expansion?} It approximates a smooth function near $x_i$ by a polynomial. The term $hf'_i$ predicts change using the slope; $h^2f''_i/2$ corrects for curvature. The remainder $O(h^4)$ is bounded by a constant times $h^4$ for small $h$ and bounded higher derivatives. Expanding on both sides gives
\begin{align*}
f_{i+1}&=f_i+h f'_i+\frac{h^2}{2}f''_i+\frac{h^3}{6}f'''_i+O(h^4),\\
f_{i-1}&=f_i-h f'_i+\frac{h^2}{2}f''_i-\frac{h^3}{6}f'''_i+O(h^4).
\end{align*}
Subtract $f_i$ from the first line and divide by $h$:
\[
f_{i+1}-f_i=hf'_i+\tfrac12h^2f''_i+\cdots
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\frac{f_{i+1}-f_i}{h}=f'_i+\tfrac12hf''_i+\cdots.
\]
The second expansion gives the backward formula; subtracting the expansions gives the centered formula. Error below means approximation minus exact derivative:
\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{lll}\toprule
Method & Approximation to $f'_i$ & Leading error\\\midrule
Forward & $(f_{i+1}-f_i)/h$ & $+(h/2)f''_i$\\[5pt]
Backward & $(f_i-f_{i-1})/h$ & $-(h/2)f''_i$\\[5pt]
Centered & $(f_{i+1}-f_{i-1})/(2h)$ & $+(h^2/6)f'''_i$\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{center}
\textbf{Why $2h$?} The centered numerator spans from $x_i-h$ to $x_i+h$, a distance $2h$. Dividing it by $h$ would double the derivative.

\textbf{Why use the centered formula here?} For smooth interior fields with values on both sides, symmetry cancels the $O(h)$ term. The resulting $O(h^2)$ error is the reason for choosing it in this introductory diffusion and Poisson discretization. It does not guarantee stability for advection-dominated flows or shocks; a complete time-dependent scheme must also satisfy stability and boundary requirements.

\textbf{Boundary distinction.} At $i=0$ there is no stored $f_{-1}$. A centered stencil therefore needs ghost values or a different boundary formula. A second-order one-sided derivative is $(-3f_0+4f_1-f_2)/(2h)$. For cavity walls, Section~8 derives the boundary treatment.

\topic{7.1}{Worked examples: accuracy and curvature}\label{sec:fd-errors}
Take $f(x)=x^3$ at $x=1$. The exact slope is $f'(1)=3$. For $h=0.1$, the samples are $f(0.9)=0.729$, $f(1)=1$, and $f(1.1)=1.331$:
\[
D_+f=\frac{1.331-1}{0.1}=3.31,\quad
D_-f=\frac{1-0.729}{0.1}=2.71,\quad
D_0f=\frac{1.331-0.729}{0.2}=3.01.
\]
\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}\toprule
$h$ & Forward & Backward & Centered & $|D_0f-3|$\\\midrule
0.10 & 3.3100 & 2.7100 & 3.0100 & 0.0100\\
0.05 & 3.1525 & 2.8525 & 3.0025 & 0.0025\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{center}
Halving $h$ reduces the centered error by a factor of four here. This is what second-order truncation error means: approximately $C h^2$ for a smooth fixed function as $h$ decreases. It does not mean two decimal places or two iterations. At extremely small spacing, roundoff and subtractive cancellation can become important.

\textbf{Derivative order versus accuracy order.} A first derivative is a slope; a second derivative is curvature. Either can have second-order accuracy: this refers to how its error decreases with grid spacing, not which derivative we calculate.

\textbf{Second derivative: add instead of subtract.} Adding the Taylor expansions eliminates the odd powers:
\[
f_{i+1}+f_{i-1}=2f_i+h^2f''_i+\frac{h^4}{12}f^{(4)}_i+O(h^6).
\]
Thus
\[
\boxed{D_{xx}f_i=\frac{f_{i+1}-2f_i+f_{i-1}}{h^2}
=f''_i+\frac{h^2}{12}f^{(4)}_i+O(h^4).}
\]
This measures how the slope changes, or curvature. For $f=x^4$ at $x=1$ with $h=0.1$, it gives $(1.4641-2+0.6561)/0.01=12.02$, compared with the exact value $12$. At $h=0.05$ it gives $12.005$: the error again decreases fourfold.

\textbf{Small executable example (independent of the CFD run)}
\begin{lstlisting}
for h in (0.1, 0.05):
    f = lambda x: x**3
    forward = (f(1+h) - f(1)) / h
    backward = (f(1) - f(1-h)) / h
    centered = (f(1+h) - f(1-h)) / (2*h)
    curvature = ((1+h)**4 - 2 + (1-h)**4) / h**2
    print(h, forward, backward, centered, curvature)
\end{lstlisting}
\checkpoint{For $f=x^2$, centered first and second derivatives are exact in exact arithmetic. Why is that example alone insufficient to demonstrate the observed order of a general derivative approximation?}

\subsection*{Measuring accuracy in the notebook}
For errors $e_i=D_hf_i-f'(x_i)$ at $M$ interior nodes, the notebook uses the RMS norm $E_h=\sqrt{\sum_i e_i^2/M}$. If $E_h\approx Ch^p$, halving $h$ gives
\[
p_{\rm obs}=\frac{\log(E_h/E_{h/2})}{\log2}.
\]
The cubic example above gives $\log(0.01/0.0025)/\log2=2$. The notebook tests $\sin(\pi x)$ on five grids and compares its numerical derivative with $\pi\cos(\pi x)$; it then repeats a boundary check for $e^x$. Known derivatives reveal errors that a smooth-looking curve can hide.

\topic{7.2}{Taylor expansions and the two-dimensional stencil}\label{sec:fd-grid}
\textbf{Field samples on the grid.} At a fixed time, sample a smooth scalar field $f(x,y)$ on a rectangular grid. Coordinates are $x_i=x_0+i\Delta x$, $y_j=y_0+j\Delta y$. We store $f_{j,i}=f(x_i,y_j)$: array row $j$ corresponds to $y$, and array column $i$ corresponds to $x$.

\textbf{Expansion in the horizontal direction.} Name the center $C=f(x_i,y_j)$, its east neighbor $E=f(x_i+\Delta x,y_j)$, and its west neighbor $W=f(x_i-\Delta x,y_j)$. Taylor expansion about the same center gives
\begin{align*}
E&=C+\Delta x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
+\frac{\Delta x^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}
+\frac{\Delta x^3}{6}\frac{\partial^3f}{\partial x^3}+O(\Delta x^4),\\
W&=C-\Delta x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
+\frac{\Delta x^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}
-\frac{\Delta x^3}{6}\frac{\partial^3f}{\partial x^3}+O(\Delta x^4).
\end{align*}
All derivatives on these right sides are evaluated at $(x_i,y_j)$. Adding the equations cancels the odd powers:
\[
E+W=2C+\Delta x^2\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}+O(\Delta x^4).
\]
Subtract $2C$ and divide by $\Delta x^2$:
\[
\boxed{\left.\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}\right|_{(x_i,y_j)}
=\frac{E-2C+W}{\Delta x^2}+O(\Delta x^2).}
\]
This explains the coefficient $-2$: each neighbor expansion contains one center value, so their sum contains $2C$. It also explains the denominator: a second derivative requires division by the square of the spacing.

\textbf{Expansion in the vertical direction.} Let $N=f(x_i,y_j+\Delta y)$ and $S=f(x_i,y_j-\Delta y)$. The same calculation gives
\begin{align*}
N&=C+\Delta y\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}+\frac{\Delta y^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\Delta y^3}{6}\frac{\partial^3f}{\partial y^3}+O(\Delta y^4),\\
S&=C-\Delta y\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}+\frac{\Delta y^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y^2}-\frac{\Delta y^3}{6}\frac{\partial^3f}{\partial y^3}+O(\Delta y^4).
\end{align*}
The cubic terms have opposite signs and cancel on addition, just as in $x$. Thus
\[
\boxed{\left.\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y^2}\right|_{(x_i,y_j)}
=\frac{N-2C+S}{\Delta y^2}+O(\Delta y^2).}
\]
A second derivative measures curvature, not upward or downward velocity. The letters $N,S,E,W$ identify locations; they are not signed physical quantities.
\newpage
\topic{7.2 continued}{The five-point Laplacian and array indexing}
The Laplacian is defined as the sum of the two second derivatives:
\[
\nabla^2 f=\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y^2}.
\]
Substitute the two formulas just derived. Denote the resulting numerical approximation by $L_h f$:
\begin{align*}
L_hf&=\frac{E-2C+W}{\Delta x^2}+\frac{N-2C+S}{\Delta y^2}.\\
\text{If }\Delta x=\Delta y=h,\qquad
L_hf&=\frac{E-2C+W+N-2C+S}{h^2}\\
&=\boxed{\frac{E+W+N+S-4C}{h^2}}.
\end{align*}
The center coefficient is $-4$ because the $x$ and $y$ formulas each contribute $-2C$. The approximation error is $O(\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2)$ for smooth fields. A stencil lists the locations and weights in this approximation; no diagonal neighbors are needed here.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1.45cm,y=.9cm,>=stealth]
\draw[accent,thick] (-1,0)--(1,0) (0,-1)--(0,1);
\foreach \x/\y/\lab in {0/0/C,1/0/E,-1/0/W,0/1/N,0/-1/S}
{\node[draw=accent,fill=white,circle,inner sep=4pt] at (\x,\y) {$\lab$};}
\node[right] at (1.3,0) {$E=f_{j,i+1}$};
\node[above] at (0,1.25) {$N=f_{j+1,i}$};
\node[below] at (0,-1.25) {$S=f_{j-1,i}$};
\node[left] at (-1.3,0) {$W=f_{j,i-1}$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\textbf{Polynomial example.} For $f=x^2+3y^2$, center $(0.5,0.5)$, and $h=0.1$, the five values are $(C,E,W,N,S)=(1,1.11,0.91,1.33,0.73)$. Therefore
\[
\frac{E-2C+W}{h^2}=\frac{1.11-2+0.91}{0.01}=2,\qquad
\frac{N-2C+S}{h^2}=\frac{1.33-2+0.73}{0.01}=6.
\]
Their sum is $8$, equal to the exact Laplacian $2+6$. This is a derivative check, not a cavity flow solution.

\topic{7.3}{From the Poisson equation to an iteration}\label{sec:fd-system}
\textbf{Streamfunction Poisson equation.} The streamfunction equation derived in Section~5 is
\[
\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2}=-\omega.
\]
Vorticity $\omega$ is held fixed during this Poisson solve. Insert the second-difference formulas at one interior node:
\[
\frac{\psi_E-2\psi_C+\psi_W}{\Delta x^2}
+\frac{\psi_N-2\psi_C+\psi_S}{\Delta y^2}=-\omega_C.
\]
Multiply every term by $\Delta x^2\Delta y^2$ and collect the center terms:
\begin{align*}
&\Delta y^2(\psi_E+\psi_W)-2\Delta y^2\psi_C
+\Delta x^2(\psi_N+\psi_S)-2\Delta x^2\psi_C
=-\omega_C\Delta x^2\Delta y^2,\\
&2(\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2)\psi_C
=\Delta y^2(\psi_E+\psi_W)+\Delta x^2(\psi_N+\psi_S)
+\omega_C\Delta x^2\Delta y^2.
\end{align*}
Divide by the coefficient of $\psi_C$. This is an algebraic rearrangement, not yet a new time step:
\[
\psi_C=\frac{\Delta y^2(\psi_E+\psi_W)+\Delta x^2(\psi_N+\psi_S)
+\omega_C\Delta x^2\Delta y^2}{2(\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2)}.
\]
\textbf{Why iterate?} The four neighboring interior values are also unknown. Start with a guess and repeatedly use the previous sweep's neighbors. If $k$ counts these Jacobi sweeps, the update implemented by the notebook is
\begin{equation}
\psi_{j,i}^{k+1}=\frac{(\psi^k_{j,i+1}+\psi^k_{j,i-1})\Delta y^2
+(\psi^k_{j+1,i}+\psi^k_{j-1,i})\Delta x^2
+\omega_{j,i}\Delta x^2\Delta y^2}{2(\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2)}.
\label{eq:jacobi-expanded}
\end{equation}
The superscript $k$ is an iteration counter, not an exponent and not physical time. The subscript $j,i$ is a grid location. The current $\omega$ and prescribed boundary values do not change during these inner sweeps.

\textbf{Equal spacing gives a simpler row.} With $\Delta x=\Delta y=h$, multiply the discrete Poisson equation by $h^2$:
\begin{align*}
\psi_E+\psi_W+\psi_N+\psi_S-4\psi_C&=-h^2\omega_C,\\
4\psi_C-\psi_E-\psi_W-\psi_N-\psi_S&=h^2\omega_C,\\
\psi_C^{k+1}&=\tfrac14(\psi_E^k+\psi_W^k+\psi_N^k+\psi_S^k+h^2\omega_C).
\end{align*}
The positive vorticity term in the update follows from rearranging $\nabla^2\psi=-\omega$. It is not an arbitrary sign choice.

\topic{7.3 continued}{A four-node Poisson system}
\textbf{Grid, source, and boundary data.} Use the unit square with grid coordinates $0,1/3,2/3,1$ in each direction. Thus $h=1/3$, $h^2=1/9$, and there are four interior unknowns. Prescribe $\psi=0$ at every boundary node and $\omega=-2$ at each interior node. Label the unknowns by location:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
$y\backslash x$ & $0$ & $1/3$ & $2/3$ & $1$\\\hline
$1$ & $0$&$0$&$0$&$0$\\
$2/3$ & $0$&$c$&$d$&$0$\\
$1/3$ & $0$&$a$&$b$&$0$\\
$0$ & $0$&$0$&$0$&$0$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Each row comes from $4\psi_C-\psi_E-\psi_W-\psi_N-\psi_S=h^2\omega_C$. Here every right side is $(1/9)(-2)=-2/9$. Substitute the neighbors individually:
\begin{align*}
\text{At }a:\quad4a-\underbrace{b}_{E}-\underbrace{0}_{W}-\underbrace{c}_{N}-\underbrace{0}_{S}&=-2/9,\\
\text{At }b:\quad4b-\underbrace{0}_{E}-\underbrace{a}_{W}-\underbrace{d}_{N}-\underbrace{0}_{S}&=-2/9,\\
\text{At }c:\quad4c-\underbrace{d}_{E}-\underbrace{0}_{W}-\underbrace{0}_{N}-\underbrace{a}_{S}&=-2/9,\\
\text{At }d:\quad4d-\underbrace{0}_{E}-\underbrace{c}_{W}-\underbrace{0}_{N}-\underbrace{b}_{S}&=-2/9.
\end{align*}
Now choose the unknown vector in the order $(a,b,c,d)$. Read each equation's coefficients in that same order:
\[
\begin{array}{rcr}
4a-b-c+0d&=&-2/9,\\
-a+4b+0c-d&=&-2/9,\\
-a+0b+4c-d&=&-2/9,\\
0a-b-c+4d&=&-2/9.
\end{array}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\begin{bmatrix}
4&-1&-1&0\\-1&4&0&-1\\-1&0&4&-1\\0&-1&-1&4
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}a\\b\\c\\d\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}-2/9\\-2/9\\-2/9\\-2/9\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The diagonal $4$ belongs to the center. A coefficient $-1$ belongs to an interior cardinal neighbor. A zero means that unknown is not a neighbor in that row. In $A\boldsymbol q=\boldsymbol b$, $A$ is this coefficient matrix, $\boldsymbol q=(a,b,c,d)^T$ is the unknown vector, and $\boldsymbol b$ is the known right side. This $\boldsymbol b$ is a vector, distinct from the scalar grid value named $b$.

\textbf{If a wall value were nonzero}, it would enter the known right side. For example, west boundary $g_W$ and south boundary $g_S$ at node $a$ give $4a-b-c=-2/9+g_W+g_S$. The wall data have not been ignored; they are zero in the chosen example.
\newpage
\topic{7.3 continued}{Solution and Jacobi convergence}
\textbf{Exact solution of the discrete system.} All four equations have identical data and symmetric positions. Setting $a=b=c=d=s$ reduces each row to $4s-s-s=2s=-2/9$, giving $s=-1/9$. For example, the first row gives
\[
4(-1/9)-(-1/9)-(-1/9)=-2/9.
\]
This satisfies every row; the discrete Dirichlet Poisson matrix is nonsingular, so this is the unique solution. Prescribed $\omega=-2$ is a demonstration input, not the vorticity from a complete no-slip cavity run.

\textbf{Jacobi iteration.} Isolate each diagonal term and use only the preceding sweep's neighbors:
\begin{align*}
a^{k+1}&=\tfrac14(b^k+c^k-2/9),&
b^{k+1}&=\tfrac14(a^k+d^k-2/9),\\
c^{k+1}&=\tfrac14(a^k+d^k-2/9),&
d^{k+1}&=\tfrac14(b^k+c^k-2/9).
\end{align*}
Start from $a^0=b^0=c^0=d^0=0$. The first sweep is
\[
a^1=b^1=c^1=d^1=\tfrac14(0+0-2/9)=-1/18.
\]
The second sweep uses $-1/18$ from the first sweep at both neighbors:
\[
a^2=\tfrac14(-1/18-1/18-2/9)=-1/12,
\]
and the other three entries have the same value. Continuing gives
\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{rrr}\toprule
Sweep $k$ & Each interior value & Absolute error relative to $-1/9$\\\midrule
0 & $0$ & $1/9$\\
1 & $-1/18$ & $1/18$\\
2 & $-1/12$ & $1/36$\\
3 & $-7/72$ & $1/72$\\
$\infty$ & $-1/9$ & $0$\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{center}
\textbf{Implementation.} The notebook uses \texttt{old = psi.copy()} to retain the previous sweep. Its center slice is assigned the update derived above, using four neighbor slices from \texttt{old}. Reusing a newly updated neighbor within a point-by-point loop would instead produce a Gauss--Seidel sweep.

\textbf{Inner iteration and physical time.} During these inner iterations, the streamfunction guess changes; vorticity, wall values, mesh, and physical time stay fixed. After the Poisson solve, the outer algorithm obtains velocity from streamfunction and advances vorticity in time. A new vorticity field requires another Poisson solve.
